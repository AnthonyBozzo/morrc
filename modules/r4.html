Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Structure a manuscript (IMRaD) and a structured abstract.
  • Apply ICMJE authorship criteria.
  • Choose a target journal and respond to reviewers.
  • Write a constructive peer review.

Prerequisites: F6 and R2. Not locked: you can start here.

Why this matters to youA finished project only counts once it is published. Clear writing, fair authorship and constructive reviewing are also part of your professional reputation, from residency onward.

1. IMRaD, and the order to write it in

SectionAnswersTips
IntroductionWhy did you do it?Three paragraphs: what's known, the gap, your objective or hypothesis. End with the question.
MethodsWhat did you do?Write it first, from your protocol, using the reporting checklist (F6) as a scaffold. Include enough detail to replicate, plus REB approval and registration.
ResultsWhat did you find?Flow of patients, baseline characteristics, the primary outcome with effect size and CI, then secondary outcomes and harms. No interpretation.
DiscussionWhat does it mean?Main finding in one sentence; comparison with the literature; strengths and limitations (honestly); implications; conclusion consistent with the primary result.

A practical writing order: tables and figures → methods → results → introduction → discussion → abstract → title.

Tables and figures

  • Table 1 shows baseline characteristics by group. In RCTs, don't test baseline differences with p-values (CONSORT): with proper randomization, any differences are due to chance by definition.
  • Show a flow diagram (CONSORT / STROBE) with numbers at each stage and reasons for exclusion and loss.
  • The primary outcome table gives each group's result, the between-group difference and its 95% CI.
  • Figures should be readable in greyscale, with labelled axes and units.

2. The structured abstract

Most readers, and many editors, read only the abstract. It should state the design in the title or first line; give the setting, participants and primary outcome with its time point; report the primary result with numbers, effect size and CI; include harms; and end with a conclusion that matches the primary result, without spin (A6). Respect the word limit, typically 250 to 300 words.

3. Authorship (ICMJE)

The ICMJE Recommendations require all four criteria for authorship:

  1. Substantial contributions to the conception or design of the work, or to the acquisition, analysis or interpretation of data; and
  2. Drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content; and
  3. Final approval of the version to be published; and
  4. Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work.

Contributors who don't meet all four are acknowledged, with their permission. Gift or guest authorship (adding someone for seniority) and ghost authorship (omitting someone who wrote the paper, e.g., an industry medical writer) are both unethical. Agree on authorship and order at the start and revisit it as roles change. Many journals ask for contributions using the CRediT taxonomy.

4. Choosing a journal, and responding to reviewers

  • Match the scope and audience by looking at where similar papers are published. Consider article type, word limits, open-access fees and the review timeline.
  • Predatory journals solicit by flattering email, promise rapid acceptance and charge fees without real peer review. Check indexing (MEDLINE/PubMed), use the Think. Check. Submit. checklist and ask your mentor. Publishing there can harm your CV.
  • Cover letter: the question, the main finding, why it matters to this journal's readers, and confirmation of originality, ethics and registration.
  • Responding to reviewers: thank them; reply point by point, quoting each comment; change the manuscript where possible and give page and line numbers; disagree politely with evidence; never ignore a comment.

5. Writing a peer review

COPE's Ethical Guidelines for Peer Reviewers set out the essentials: keep the manuscript confidential; declare competing interests or decline; review only within your expertise; be timely, objective and constructive; and don't use unpublished information for your own advantage. Check the journal's policy on AI tools: uploading a confidential manuscript to a generative-AI service can breach confidentiality, and many journals prohibit it.

Structure

  1. Summary (2 to 3 sentences) of the question, design and main finding, to show you understood it.
  2. Major issues affecting validity or conclusions (design, bias, analysis, spin), each with a suggested fix.
  3. Minor issues: clarity, tables, references.
  4. Confidential comments to the editor, including your recommendation (keep it out of the comments to authors).

Residents can co-review with a mentor, with the editor's permission. It is an excellent way to learn. [COMMITTEE: faculty willing to co-review with residents]
