F3. Bias, confounding and chance

Before believing any result, rule out the three alternative explanations: chance, bias and confounding.

  • Distinguish bias (systematic error) from chance (random error).
  • Describe how randomization, blinding and adjustment address each.

Prerequisites: F1 and F2 (recommended).

1. Three rival explanations for any result

When a study reports that treatment A is better than B, the difference could be real, or it could be due to:

  • Chance (random error): the play of chance in who happened to be sampled. It shrinks as sample size grows and is quantified by confidence intervals and p-values.
  • Bias (systematic error): a flaw in design, conduct or analysis that pushes results consistently in one direction. It does not shrink with sample size: a huge biased study is precisely wrong.
  • Confounding: a mixing of effects, where a third factor linked to both treatment and outcome creates or hides an association. Many classify it as a type of bias; it is singled out because it dominates observational research.

2. The bias taxonomy

The Cochrane framework groups biases in comparative studies into five families. Each one, with an orthopaedic example and its main protection:

Loss to follow-up: a rough guideA common rule of thumb is that less than 5% loss is unlikely to matter much and more than 20% is a serious threat. What really matters is whether losses differ between groups and could be related to the outcome. Test this with a worst-case sensitivity analysis: would the conclusion change if all the missing patients in one group did poorly?

3. Confounding

A confounder is associated with the exposure, independently affects the outcome, and is not on the causal pathway between them.

Worked example: confounding by indication

A chart review finds older adults treated with volar plating for distal radius fractures had better PRWE scores than those treated in a cast. But surgeons tend to offer plating to fitter, more active patients. Fitness influences both the treatment chosen and the functional outcome, so plated patients may have done better regardless of the plate. This is confounding by indication: the reason for treatment is itself prognostic. It is the dominant threat in observational studies of surgery, and can run either way (sicker patients may be steered toward or away from surgery).

Handling confounding

  • At the design stage: randomization (balances known and unknown confounders), restriction (e.g., only independent walkers), matching.
  • At the analysis stage: stratification, multivariable regression, propensity scores (module A2). These handle only confounders that were measured, and measured well; residual and unmeasured confounding always remains a possibility in observational research.

Effect modification is different

An effect modifier changes the size of a real effect across subgroups (a treatment that helps smokers more than non-smokers, say). Confounding is a nuisance to remove; effect modification is a finding to describe, if it's credible. Subgroup credibility is covered in A4 and A6.

4. Introduction to causal diagrams (DAGs)

A directed acyclic graph draws your assumptions about what causes what. It tells you what to adjust for and what to leave alone.

Two rules for now: adjust for common causes of exposure and outcome (confounders); don't adjust for mediators (when you want the total effect) or for colliders (common effects of exposure and outcome), because doing so can create bias. Free tools such as DAGitty help you draw and check DAGs.

5. Regression to the mean, natural history and placebo effects in surgery

Regression to the mean: patients with fluctuating conditions (back pain, tendinopathy, degenerative knee pain) tend to present, and be enrolled, when symptoms are at their worst. On remeasurement they tend to be closer to their usual level, even untreated. Add natural history (many conditions improve with time) and an uncontrolled before–after study will almost always “show” improvement.

Placebo effects in surgery can be large, especially for subjective outcomes like pain. Sham-controlled trials have repeatedly shown this in orthopaedics:

  • Arthroscopic lavage or debridement for knee osteoarthritis was no better than a sham procedure (Moseley et al., NEJM 2002).
  • Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy for a degenerative tear was no better than sham surgery (FIDELITY; Sihvonen et al., NEJM 2013).
  • Subacromial decompression gave no clinically important benefit over investigational arthroscopy alone (CSAW; Beard et al., Lancet 2018).

A systematic review of placebo-controlled surgical trials (Wartolowska et al., BMJ 2014) found that patients in sham arms often improved substantially, and in many trials as much as those having the real operation.

Implication for your own researchA before–after case series can't tell you whether an operation works. You need a concurrent control group, and for subjective outcomes, ideally blinding of patients and assessors.

6. Chance

Random error is quantified, not eliminated. A 95% confidence interval shows the range of effects compatible with the data. A p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if there were no true effect; it is not the probability that the result is due to chance. Three practical points:

  • Small studies are imprecise: “no significant difference” in a small trial often means “we don't know”, not “no difference”.
  • Multiplicity: test 20 independent outcomes with no true effects and, on average, one will reach p < 0.05.
  • Chance and bias are independent problems: a tiny p-value says nothing about bias.

Module F4 (Biostatistics essentials I) covers p-values, CIs and power in depth.

7. Protections, and the RoB 2 tool

ToolMainly protects againstDoes not protect against

Blinding of outcome assessorsDetection biasPerformance bias
Complete follow-up; intention-to-treatAttrition bias; bias from crossoversConfounding in observational designs
Registration and published protocolReporting biasConduct problems
Restriction, matching, adjustment, propensity scoresMeasured confoundingUnmeasured or poorly measured confounding
Larger sample sizeChanceAny bias

The Cochrane RoB 2 tool (Sterne et al., BMJ 2019) assesses randomized trials across five domains, judged per outcome: (1) the randomization process; (2) deviations from intended interventions; (3) missing outcome data; (4) measurement of the outcome; (5) selection of the reported result. Each domain is rated low risk, some concerns, or high risk. For non-randomized studies, the companion tool is ROBINS-I. You will use both in Tier 2.
