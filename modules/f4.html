Why this matters to youMost misreadings of orthopaedic papers come from three errors: treating p < 0.05 as proof, treating p > 0.05 as proof of no difference, and being impressed by relative risk reductions. This module fixes all three.

1. Describing data

  • Continuous, roughly symmetric data (age, many PROM totals in large samples): report the mean and standard deviation (SD).
  • Skewed data (length of stay, time to union, costs, blood loss): report the median and interquartile range (IQR). A few long stays drag the mean upward.
  • Categorical data (union yes/no, reoperation): report counts and percentages, always with the denominator.
  • SD vs standard error (SE): the SD describes spread between patients; the SE (SD/√n) describes the precision of the mean. Error bars showing SE look reassuringly small. Check which one a figure shows.

2. Hypothesis tests and p-values

A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as those seen if the null hypothesis (usually “no difference”) and all other model assumptions were true. In 2016, the American Statistical Association issued six principles (Wasserstein and Lazar), paraphrased here:

  1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
  2. They do not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true, or that the data arose by chance alone.
  3. Conclusions should not rest only on whether a p-value crosses a threshold such as 0.05.
  4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency (all analyses, not just the significant ones).
  5. A p-value does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
  6. By itself, a p-value is not a good measure of evidence for a model or hypothesis.
Absence of evidence is not evidence of absenceA small trial reporting “no significant difference (p = 0.30)” has often simply failed to detect a difference that may exist (Altman and Bland, BMJ 1995). Look at the confidence interval: if it includes clinically important benefit and harm, the study is inconclusive, not negative.

3. Confidence intervals and clinical importance

A 95% confidence interval (CI) gives the range of true effects reasonably compatible with the data. Narrow CIs mean precise estimates; wide CIs mean imprecise ones. The key move in appraisal is to compare the CI not only with zero (no effect) but with the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), the smallest change patients perceive as worthwhile (module A5).

Where the CI lies (higher = better)Interpretation
Entirely above the MCIDClinically important benefit
Above zero, but includes the MCIDStatistically significant; possibly important
Above zero, entirely below the MCIDStatistically significant but trivial
Includes zero and the MCIDInconclusive (imprecise)
Includes zero, excludes the MCIDImportant benefit unlikely

4. Relative vs absolute effects, NNT and NNH

For a dichotomous outcome with control event rate (CER) and experimental event rate (EER):

MeasureFormulaMeaning
Relative risk (RR)EER / CERRisk with treatment as a proportion of risk without
Relative risk reduction (RRR)1 − RRProportion of baseline risk removed
Absolute risk reduction (ARR)CER − EERRisk difference: what the patient actually gains
Number needed to treat (NNT)1 / ARR (round up)Patients treated for one to benefit
Number needed to harm (NNH)1 / absolute risk increasePatients treated for one to be harmed
Odds ratio (OR)[a/b] / [c/d]Ratio of odds; close to RR only when the outcome is uncommon
Worked example (illustrative numbers)

The COTS trial (JBJS Am 2007) randomized displaced midshaft clavicle fractures to plate fixation or a sling and found fewer nonunions with plating. To practise the arithmetic we use round illustrative numbers, not the trial's data: nonunion in 15 of 100 with a sling and 3 of 100 with a plate.

  • CER = 0.15; EER = 0.03; RR = 0.03/0.15 = 0.20; RRR = 80%.
  • ARR = 0.15 − 0.03 = 0.12 (12 percentage points); NNT = 1/0.12 = 8.3, rounded up to 9.
  • If plating caused a hardware-related reoperation in 10 of 100 vs 0 of 100 with a sling, the NNH would be 1/0.10 = 10. Benefit and harm now sit on the same scale for the shared decision.

Exercise: look up the COTS paper and compute the real NNT for nonunion from its reported numbers.

Same RRR, very different valueA 50% RRR turns 2% into 1% (ARR 1%, NNT 100) or 40% into 20% (ARR 20%, NNT 5). Relative effects travel well between populations; absolute effects depend on baseline risk and drive decisions. Laupacis, Sackett and Roberts introduced the NNT (NEJM 1988), and Altman showed how to give it a confidence interval (BMJ 1998).

5. Type I and type II errors, and power

Truly no differenceTruly a difference
Study finds a differenceType I error (false positive), probability α (usually 0.05)Correct (probability = power, 1 − β)
Study finds no differenceCorrectType II error (false negative), probability β (often 0.20)

Power rises with larger sample size, larger true effect, less variability, a more lenient α, and more events for binary outcomes. Many orthopaedic trials are underpowered, so their “negative” results are inconclusive. Sample-size calculation is covered in A6.

ProportionsChi-square (Fisher's exact if expected counts are small)McNemarChi-square

To adjust for other variables, use regression: linear for continuous outcomes, logistic for binary, Cox for time-to-event. Bilateral cases or repeated measures need methods that account for clustering (A6). Bring your question to a statistician before data collection.
