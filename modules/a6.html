Why this matters to youAn underpowered resident project can't answer its question, and reviewers will say so. These four skills turn you from someone who reads p-values into someone who can judge, and design, a credible trial.

1. Sample size: the ingredients

  • α (type I error, usually 0.05 two-sided) and power (1 − β, usually 80% or 90%).
  • The target difference: the smallest difference worth detecting, ideally the MCID (A5) or a clinically important risk difference. Don't use an optimistic guess just because it shrinks the sample.
  • Variability: the SD for continuous outcomes (from pilot data or prior trials), or the control-group event rate for binary outcomes.
  • Attrition: inflate n as n / (1 − expected loss).
  • Clustering (patients within surgeons, or two limbs per patient): multiply by the design effect, 1 + (m − 1) × ICC, where m is the cluster size.

Formulas used by the calculator (per group, equal allocation)

  • Continuous: n = 2 (z1−α/2 + z1−β)² σ² / δ²

Hand-worked examples (these are the calculator's test cases)

Continuous: δ = 5 points, σ = 10, α = 0.05, power 80%: z values 1.960 and 0.842; n = 2 × (2.802)² × 100 / 25 = 62.8, so 63 per group. With 10% attrition: 63 / 0.9 = 70 per group.

Binary: 20% vs 10% reoperation, α = 0.05, power 80%: p̄ = 0.15; [1.960 × √0.255 + 0.842 × √0.25]² / 0.01 = (0.990 + 0.421)² / 0.01 = 199.0, so 199 per group.

Post hoc power is uninformativeCalculating “observed power” after a non-significant result just restates the p-value. Interpret the confidence interval instead (F4).

2. The fragility index

Walsh et al. (J Clin Epidemiol 2014) defined the fragility index (FI) as the minimum number of patients whose status would have to change from non-event to event, in the group with fewer events, to turn a statistically significant result (Fisher's exact test, p < 0.05) non-significant.

  • A small FI (e.g., 1 to 3) means the conclusion hinges on a handful of events.
  • Compare the FI with the number lost to follow-up: if more patients were lost than the FI, their missing outcomes could plausibly reverse the conclusion.
  • Limitations: it applies only to binary outcomes and 1:1 comparisons, is tied to the arbitrary 0.05 threshold, and largely re-expresses the p-value and sample size. Use it as a reading aid, not a verdict.

3. Non-inferiority and equivalence trials

These trials ask whether a new treatment (often cheaper, less invasive or with fewer complications) is not unacceptably worse than the standard. The key design element is the non-inferiority margin (Δ), the largest loss of efficacy that is clinically acceptable. It should be justified before the trial, from clinical reasoning and the standard's established effect.

  • Non-inferiority is shown if the whole CI for the difference (new − standard, higher = better) lies above −Δ.
  • Analyze by both ITT and per-protocol: in non-inferiority trials, ITT can bias toward similarity (crossovers dilute differences), so results should agree.
  • A non-significant superiority trial does not show equivalence.
  • Report with the CONSORT extension for non-inferiority and equivalence trials (Piaggio et al., JAMA 2012).

4. Clustering, bilateral cases and multiplicity

  • Unit of analysis: two knees from one patient aren't independent. Analyzing limbs as if they were independent overstates precision. Use one limb per patient, or methods for correlated data (mixed models, GEE).
  • Surgeon clustering: outcomes cluster within surgeons and centres, so account for it in sample size and analysis.
  • Multiplicity: many outcomes, time points and subgroups inflate false positives. Prespecify one primary outcome, and treat others as secondary or exploratory, with correction where appropriate.

5. Spin

Boutron et al. (JAMA 2010) found spin in a large proportion of reports of RCTs with non-significant primary outcomes. Common strategies:

  • Focusing on statistically significant secondary outcomes or subgroups.
  • Highlighting within-group improvement (“both groups improved significantly”) instead of the between-group comparison.
  • Interpreting non-significance as equivalence or safety.
  • Concluding with recommendations not supported by the primary result.

Antidote: read the registered primary outcome and its CI first, then compare the abstract's conclusion with it.
