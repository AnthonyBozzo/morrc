Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • convert a clinical uncertainty into a PICO(T) question;
  • apply the FINER criteria to judge whether a question is feasible and important;
  • distinguish therapy, harm, diagnosis and prognosis questions and match each to the best study design;
  • run a focused literature search (PubMed, MeSH, Embase) and decide whether a true evidence gap exists.
Why this matters to youA vague question gives a vague search, an unfocused study and an unpublishable paper. The minutes spent sharpening a question save months later. The same skill makes journal club faster: the first thing to ask of any paper is “What exactly was the question?”

1. From clinical uncertainty to an answerable question

Clinical uncertainty usually shows up as a hallway remark: “At her age, I'm not sure the plate makes a difference.” Before you can search or study it, you need to know what kind of question you have.

  • Background questions ask for general knowledge: What is the blood supply of the femoral head? How does a locking plate work? Textbooks and narrative reviews answer them.
  • Foreground questions ask for specific evidence to inform a decision about a patient or group: In older adults with a displaced distal radius fracture, does plating improve function compared with casting? These are what clinical research answers, and they are the focus of MORRC.

As residents gain experience, more of their questions become foreground questions. A good foreground question has four or five parts, described next.

2. PICO(T): the anatomy of a foreground question

The PICO format, popularized by Richardson and colleagues as “the well-built clinical question”, breaks a question into components that map directly onto search terms and study eligibility criteria.

ElementAsk yourselfWeakStrong
PopulationWho exactly? Age, condition, severity, settingElderly patients with wrist fracturesIndependent adults aged 65+ with a displaced distal radius fracture
Intervention / exposureWhich specific treatment, test or exposure?SurgeryVolar locking plate fixation
ComparisonCompared with what realistic alternative?NothingClosed reduction and cast immobilization
OutcomeWhat matters to the patient, and how is it measured?Better resultsPatient-rated wrist pain and function (PRWE)
TimeWhen is the outcome assessed?(not stated)At 12 months
Worked example: hallway to question

Hallway: “Is it worth plating wrists in old people?”

PICOT: In independent adults aged 65 or older with a displaced distal radius fracture (P), does volar locking plate fixation (I), compared with closed reduction and casting (C), improve patient-rated wrist pain and function on the PRWE (O) at 12 months (T)?

Notice how each element becomes an eligibility criterion or a search concept. This exact comparison has been studied in randomized trials, including Arora et al. (JBJS Am 2011) in patients aged 65 and older. Finding that paper is the next step, not designing a new study.

Tips. Keep co-interventions equal across arms (the same rehabilitation for both groups), choose a comparator that reflects real practice, and resist putting two questions in one sentence. For harm and prognosis questions, “I” is an exposure or prognostic factor and there may be no explicit comparison.

3. Question types and the designs that answer them

The type of question sets the ideal study design. Getting this right is half of critical appraisal: an excellent cohort study is still the wrong tool for a therapy question if a trial is feasible.

TypeAsksOrthopaedic exampleBest primary design
TherapyDoes an intervention improve outcomes compared with an alternative?THA vs hemiarthroplasty for displaced femoral neck fractureRandomized controlled trial (and systematic reviews of RCTs)
HarmDoes an exposure cause an adverse outcome?Long-term bisphosphonates and atypical femoral fracturesRCT if feasible; usually large cohort or case-control studies, because harms are rare or delayed
DiagnosisHow accurately does a test identify a condition?MRI for occult scaphoid fracture; synovial markers for periprosthetic joint infectionCross-sectional study of consecutive, representative patients, all receiving an independent reference standard
PrognosisWhat is the likely course, and what predicts it?Survival after pathological fracture from skeletal metastasesInception cohort, followed from a common, early point in the disease
Common trap“Predictors of nonunion” (prognosis) and “Does smoking cause nonunion?” (harm, a causal question) are different questions. Prediction does not need causal factors, but causal questions need careful control of confounding (module F3).

4. Choosing outcomes: primary, secondary, patient-important

  • One primary outcome, prespecified (ideally in a trial registry). It drives the sample size, and naming it in advance protects against cherry-picking whichever of many outcomes reaches p < 0.05.
  • Secondary outcomes add context (complications, radiographs, return to work, cost) but are interpreted cautiously, as hypothesis-generating unless they too were powered.
  • Patient-important outcomes beat surrogates. Radiographic alignment, union on CT or implant migration are attractive because they are objective and quick, but they matter only if they reliably predict what patients feel and do. Prefer validated patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) such as PRWE or DASH for the wrist, and Oxford Hip/Knee or WOMAC for arthroplasty, plus hard events (reoperation, mortality). Module A5 covers PROMs in depth.
  • Reoperation as an outcome is common in orthopaedics. It is objective and important, but it depends on surgeon thresholds, so define it carefully (planned vs unplanned, which procedures count).

5. FINER: is this question worth answering?

Hulley and colleagues' FINER criteria (Designing Clinical Research) are a quick screen before you invest in a project.

CriterionKey questionsOrthopaedic red flag
FeasibleEnough patients, time, expertise, funding? Measurable outcome?A single-centre RCT of a rare tumour needing 400 patients
InterestingTo you, your mentor and your field?A project chosen only because a dataset exists
NovelDoes it confirm, refute or extend previous findings?A fourth small meta-analysis of the same five trials
EthicalEquipoise? Acceptable risk? REB approval achievable?Randomizing to an option most surgeons consider inferior
RelevantWould the answer change practice, policy or future research?An outcome no patient or surgeon would act on
Resident reality checkFor a first project with a 1–2 year horizon, feasible usually decides it. Retrospective cohorts, well-done systematic reviews, and joining an existing trial network are realistic first steps. A new multicentre RCT usually isn't.

6. Searching efficiently and finding a true evidence gap

  1. Start with pre-appraised and synthesized evidence: recent systematic reviews (Cochrane Library, PubMed with a systematic review filter) and clinical practice guidelines (e.g., AAOS guidelines).
  2. Build the search from your PICO. Search P and I (and sometimes C); outcomes are often poorly indexed, so don't search O at first. Within each concept, combine synonyms with OR; combine concepts with AND.
  3. Use controlled vocabulary plus free text. MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in Embase group synonyms, but the newest records may not be indexed yet. Use truncation (fracture*) and phrase searching.
  4. Narrow sensibly: PubMed Clinical Queries or publication-type filters (e.g., Randomized Controlled Trial), with care, because filters can miss mis-indexed studies.
  5. Check trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP) and PROSPERO for ongoing trials and reviews. Your “gap” may already be filled by a study recruiting now.
  6. Ask a librarian. McGill Library's health sciences librarians help with search strategies. For a systematic review, a librarian-built, peer-reviewed search is the standard.
Example PubMed search
("Radius Fractures"[MeSH] OR "distal radius fracture*" OR "wrist fracture*") AND ("Bone Plates"[MeSH] OR "volar plat*" OR "locking plate*") AND (cast* OR nonoperative OR "non-operative" OR conservative) AND (aged OR elderly OR "older adult*")

Each line in brackets is one PICO concept; OR broadens within a concept and AND narrows across concepts.

A true evidence gap exists when a recent, good-quality review leaves important uncertainty (low-certainty evidence, a missing population, an untested comparator or outcome) and nobody is already filling it. Write the gap as one sentence: “Despite X trials, no study has compared A vs B in population P on outcome O.” That sentence becomes the last line of your introduction.
