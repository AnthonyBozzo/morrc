R6. Research integrity and open science

Fabrication, falsification and plagiarism are rare. Questionable research practices, undisclosed conflicts and careless AI use are not. Learn the line, the tools that keep you on the right side of it, and where to go when something is wrong.

Why this matters to youYour name on a paper is a promise that the work is honest. Orthopaedics has close industry ties and a publish-or-perish culture, so integrity skills protect patients, colleagues and your career.

1. Misconduct and questionable research practices

  • Fabrication: making up data or results.
  • Falsification: manipulating research materials, equipment, processes, data or images so the record doesn't reflect what happened (including selectively dropping inconvenient patients).
  • Plagiarism: presenting others' words, ideas or work without credit. Copying your own published text without disclosure (self-plagiarism or duplicate publication) is also a breach.

Canada's Tri-Agency Framework: Responsible Conduct of Research (2021) also lists breaches such as mismanaging conflicts of interest, misattributing authorship, misrepresenting credentials, and redundant publication. McGill's Regulation on the Conduct of Research applies these at McGill.

Questionable research practices (QRPs)

  • P-hacking: trying analyses, outcomes, covariates or subgroups until p < 0.05. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (2011) showed how such “researcher degrees of freedom” can make false positives very likely.
  • HARKing (Kerr 1998): Hypothesizing After the Results are Known, i.e., presenting a post hoc finding as if it were planned.
  • Selective reporting and outcome switching: changing the primary outcome or omitting non-significant ones.
  • Optional stopping: checking results repeatedly and stopping when significant.
Why most published findings may be falseIoannidis (PLoS Med 2005) argued that findings are less likely to be true when studies are small, effects are small, many relationships are tested, designs and analyses are flexible, financial interests are strong and the field is “hot”. Much orthopaedic research fits several of these.

2. Conflicts of interest

A conflict of interest exists when a secondary interest (financial, professional or personal) could unduly influence, or appear to influence, judgment about a primary interest (patient welfare, research validity).

  • Financial: consulting, royalties, speaker fees, stock, patents, travel support, research grants (to you or your institution).
  • Non-financial: intellectual commitment to a technique you developed, academic rivalry, personal relationships.
  • Management: disclose (journals use the ICMJE disclosure form), recuse yourself from decisions, have an independent statistician analyze the data, and make sure contracts guarantee the investigators' right to publish regardless of results.

Disclosure doesn't eliminate bias; it lets readers judge it. When in doubt, disclose.

3. Open science

  • Preregistration of trials (required, R2), systematic reviews (PROSPERO) and, increasingly, observational studies (OSF, ClinicalTrials.gov). It limits HARKing and outcome switching.
  • Registered reports: the journal peer reviews the protocol and commits to publish regardless of results.
  • Data sharing: the ICMJE requires a data-sharing statement for clinical trials. Plan sharing at consent and in the REB application.
  • Preprints (e.g., medRxiv) speed dissemination. Check the target journal's policy and present the work clearly as not yet peer-reviewed.
  • Code and materials: share analysis scripts and data dictionaries (R3) so others can reproduce the work.

4. Corrections and retractions

Honest errors happen. If you discover one in your published work, tell your co-authors and the journal promptly: a correction for errors that don't change conclusions, a retraction for errors or misconduct that invalidate them. Retracting your own paper for honest error is respected, not punished. Before citing a paper, check whether it has been retracted (the Retraction Watch database is integrated into several reference tools).

5. Generative AI in research and writing

  • AI tools can't be authors: they can't approve a manuscript or be accountable (ICMJE, COPE).
  • Disclose AI use as the journal requires (often in the methods or acknowledgements).
  • You are responsible for every word and citation. AI tools can produce plausible but fabricated references and facts, so verify everything.
  • Never enter patient information, confidential manuscripts under review or unpublished data into public AI tools (F5, R4).
  • AI-generated images or data presented as real are fabrication.

7. Worked example: how p-hacking happens without anyone meaning to

A fictional resident project, used for teaching.

Fictional project
A resident compares two approaches for total hip arthroplasty in 120 patients. The planned outcome, the hip PROM at 12 months, shows no significant difference. Over the next month, guided by well-meaning suggestions, the team tries: the 6-week PROM instead; excluding patients with BMI over 40; a different PROM subscale; adjusting for different covariates; and analysing only patients under 70. The under-70 analysis of the 6-week score gives p = 0.03, and that becomes the abstract's headline.

What went wrong? Each choice seemed reasonable, but together they created many analyses. If there is no true effect and the team runs about 20 roughly independent analyses at α = 0.05, the chance that at least one is “significant” is 1 − 0.9520 ≈ 64%. Simmons and colleagues (2011) called this flexibility “researcher degrees of freedom”.

What should happen instead?

  1. Report the prespecified primary analysis as the main result, whatever it shows.
  2. Label additional analyses as exploratory and report how many were done.
  3. Protect yourself in future with a registered protocol and statistical analysis plan (R2), written before you see outcome data.
  4. Remember that a well-done study with a null result is publishable and useful, and that the honest version is the one you can defend at Research Day.
How common is misconduct?In a meta-analysis of surveys, Fanelli (PLoS One 2009) found that about 2% of scientists admitted to having fabricated, falsified or modified data at least once, and many more admitted questionable research practices. Most integrity problems are not dramatic fraud, but the small, unrecorded decisions in this example.
