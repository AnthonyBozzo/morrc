Prerequisites: F2 and F3 recommended. Not locked: you can start here.

Why this matters to youReporting checklists are the fastest appraisal tool you have, and the best writing scaffold for your own papers (R4). Most journals now require one at submission.

1. Why reporting guidelines exist

A study can't be appraised, replicated or included in a meta-analysis if key details are missing: how randomization was done, who was blinded, how many patients were lost. Reporting guidelines are consensus checklists of the minimum information a paper of a given design should contain. The EQUATOR Network (Enhancing the QUAlity and Transparency Of health Research, equator-network.org) indexes them.

Key distinctionReporting guidelines describe what to report, not how to conduct a study, and they are not quality scores. A perfectly reported trial can still be badly designed. Risk-of-bias tools (RoB 2, ROBINS-I, QUADAS-2) judge conduct.

3. Poor reporting vs poor conduct

“Not reported” doesn't mean “not done”. Before concluding that a trial didn't conceal allocation:

  1. Check the protocol, the trial registry record and any supplementary appendix.
  2. Look for a companion design paper (large orthopaedic trials such as FAITH and HEALTH published their methods separately).
  3. When appraising, record “unclear” or “some concerns” rather than assuming the worst, and say why.

Conversely, polished reporting can hide poor conduct. A CONSORT diagram shows the losses but doesn't fix them.

4. A structured strategy for reading a paper

  1. Triage (2 minutes): title and abstract. What is the question (PICO)? What design? Is it relevant to my patient or project? Note that abstracts are where spin concentrates, so don't stop there.
  2. Methods first (10 minutes): eligibility, interventions, primary outcome and time point, randomization and concealment, blinding, sample-size calculation, analysis population (ITT?), registration.
  3. Results: the flow diagram (losses by group), the baseline table, the primary outcome with effect size and 95% CI, absolute effects, and harms.
  4. Discussion last: do the conclusions match the primary result? Are limitations acknowledged? Compare with the registered outcomes.
  5. Applicability: would my patients have been eligible? Are the surgeons, setting and implants similar to ours?
Journal club tipBring the relevant checklist (e.g., CONSORT plus the non-pharmacologic extension) and a risk-of-bias tool. Fill in the checklist first; discussion is faster when facts are separated from judgments.

5. CONSORT for Abstracts

Hopewell and colleagues (PLoS Med 2008) defined the minimum content of a trial abstract. Key items: the title identifies the trial as randomized; trial design; participants (eligibility and setting); interventions; specific objective; clearly defined primary outcome; how participants were allocated; blinding; numbers randomized and analysed in each group; the primary outcome result for each group with effect size and precision; important harms; conclusions; trial registration; and funding.
