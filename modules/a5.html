A5. Patient-reported outcomes in orthopaedics

Patient-reported outcomes are the primary outcome of most orthopaedic trials. Learn how they are validated, what an MCID really is, and how to decide whether a difference matters.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Explain validity, reliability and responsiveness.
  • Define MCID, PASS and SCB and how each is derived.
  • Judge whether a statistically significant difference is clinically important.

Why this matters to you“Statistically significant” is the beginning of the question, not the end. Interpreting PROM differences against the MCID, correctly, is what lets you tell a patient whether an operation is worth it.

Many orthopaedic “scores” mix clinician-rated and patient-reported items (e.g., the Harris Hip Score, the Constant score). Clinician-rated items are open to detection bias when assessors aren't blinded (A1).

2. Measurement properties (COSMIN)

The COSMIN initiative standardized terminology and methods for evaluating measurement instruments:

  • Reliability: test–retest (intraclass correlation coefficient), internal consistency (Cronbach's α, only meaningful for unidimensional scales), and measurement error (standard error of measurement, smallest detectable change).
  • Validity: content validity (do the items cover what matters to patients? the most important property), structural validity, construct validity (hypothesis testing) and criterion validity.
  • Responsiveness: the ability to detect change over time.
  • Interpretability: floor and ceiling effects (Terwee et al. 2007 suggested more than 15% of respondents at the lowest or highest score as problematic) and the availability of MCID values.
Translation matters in MontrealA PROM validated in English isn't automatically valid in French. Use versions with documented cross-cultural adaptation (forward and back translation, cognitive testing), ideally validated in Canadian French where available.

3. MCID, PASS and friends

The minimal clinically important difference was defined by Jaeschke, Singer and Guyatt (1989) as the smallest difference in score that patients perceive as beneficial and that would mandate a change in management.

  • Anchor-based methods link score changes to an external anchor, such as a global rating of change (“a little better”). They are preferred because they reflect patients' perspective.
  • Distribution-based methods use statistical properties (e.g., 0.5 SD, SEM). They describe change beyond measurement error, not importance to patients.
  • MCIDs vary by method, population, baseline severity and follow-up. Copay et al. (Spine J 2007) showed how widely values differ for the same instrument, so quote the source and context of any MCID.
  • Within-patient vs between-group: an MCID for individual change isn't automatically the threshold for a meaningful difference between groups in a trial. Be explicit about which you use.
  • PASS (patient acceptable symptom state; Tubach et al. 2005) is the score above which patients consider themselves well. SCB (substantial clinical benefit) is a larger threshold for a clearly worthwhile improvement.

4. Interpreting PROM differences in trials

  1. Find the between-group difference and its 95% CI at the primary time point.
  2. Compare the CI with 0 and with the MCID (the visualizer below and module F4).
  3. Look at responder analyses (proportion achieving the MCID or PASS in each arm). These are intuitive and allow an NNT, but dichotomizing loses information and thresholds can be cherry-picked, so they should be prespecified.
  4. Check for missing PROM data. PROM completion often falls over time, and patients doing badly may respond less often.
  5. Check ceiling effects. If many patients score the maximum, true differences between good outcomes are invisible.
Worked example (illustrative)

A trial reports a 3-point difference in Oxford Knee Score (95% CI 1.5 to 4.5) favouring a new implant, and the authors call it significant. If we assume, for this example, a between-group threshold of 5 points, the whole CI lies below the threshold, so the difference is statistically significant but unlikely to be important to patients.

5. Choosing a PROM for your study

  • Start from the construct you need to measure (pain? function? return to sport?) and the population.
  • Check measurement properties in the target population using COSMIN-based systematic reviews.
  • Prefer instruments used in core outcome sets and in comparable trials, so your results can be pooled.
  • Consider respondent burden, available languages, licensing and fees, and whether your REDCap instrument library already has it (R3).
  • Prespecify the primary PROM, time point, MCID source and responder definitions in the protocol (R2).
