Why this matters to youThe protocol is your R4 capstone and the single best predictor of whether your project finishes. Every hour spent here saves weeks with the REB, the statistician and reviewers.

1. The protocol as a contract

A protocol states in advance what you will do, so that readers (and you) can tell planned analyses from post hoc ones. It is also what the REB, funders and journals review. Use SPIRIT for trials (SPIRIT 2013, updated as SPIRIT 2025) and the relevant reporting guideline (STROBE, STARD, PRISMA-P) as the scaffold for other designs.

2. The statistical analysis plan

Gamble et al. (JAMA 2017) published guidance on the content of SAPs. Your SAP should prespecify:

  • The primary analysis (population: ITT, per-protocol; model; covariates such as stratification factors).
  • Missing-data handling (e.g., multiple imputation) and sensitivity analyses.
  • Secondary outcomes and how multiplicity will be handled.
  • Subgroup analyses, with hypothesized direction and interaction tests (A4).
  • For clustered or bilateral data, how correlation is handled (A6).

Involve a statistician before the protocol is final. [COMMITTEE: link to departmental or institute biostatistics consult service]

3. Registration

  • The ICMJE requires clinical trials to be registered in a public registry before the first patient is enrolled as a condition of publication (De Angelis et al., 2004). Use ClinicalTrials.gov or another WHO-recognized registry, such as ISRCTN.
  • Systematic reviews: register in PROSPERO before screening starts.
  • Observational studies: registration is not mandatory but increasingly encouraged (ClinicalTrials.gov, OSF). It protects against accusations of data dredging.
  • Keep the registry record updated; reviewers will compare the registered outcomes with your paper (A1, R6).

4. Pilot and feasibility studies

Many resident projects are best framed as feasibility studies for a larger trial. Their objectives are recruitment and consent rates, retention, adherence, outcome completion and process issues, not effectiveness. Prespecify progression criteria (e.g., “recruit at least 2 patients per site per month and retain at least 85% at 6 months”). Report with the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility trials (Eldridge et al., BMJ 2016). Effect estimates from pilots are imprecise and shouldn't be used uncritically to power the main trial.

5. Grant writing and funding

  • Specific aims first: one page: the problem, the gap, the objective, aims with hypotheses, and the expected impact. Reviewers often decide on this page.
  • Significance, approach, feasibility: show pilot data, the team's track record, access to patients and realistic recruitment.
  • Budget: coordinator time, data management, statistician, PROM licences, open-access fees. Justify every line.
  • Lay summary in plain language. Patient partners strengthen applications (CIHR's Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research).
  • Sources: CIHR, FRQS (Fonds de recherche du Québec – Santé), orthopaedic foundations and subspecialty societies, and departmental and hospital-foundation seed grants. [COMMITTEE: list McGill Ortho / MUHC / JGH resident grants, amounts and deadlines]

6. Choosing outcomes and running randomization

Outcomes

  • One primary outcome, with its instrument and time point (e.g., Olerud–Molander Ankle Score at 12 months). It drives the sample size and the conclusion.
  • Prefer outcomes that matter to patients (function, pain, reoperation, return to work) over surrogates (radiographic union, implant position), unless the study is a pilot or a mechanistic study.
  • Check whether a core outcome set exists for your condition (the COMET Initiative database) so your results can be pooled later (A4).
  • Define adverse events and how they will be collected and graded; don't leave them to chance chart review.

Randomization logistics

  • Generate the sequence by computer, usually in permuted blocks of varying size, stratified by centre and one or two key prognostic factors.
  • Conceal allocation with central web-based randomization (REDCap has a randomization module, R3) rather than envelopes, which can be held up to the light or opened out of order.
  • Randomize as late as possible (e.g., in the operating room once eligibility is confirmed) to reduce post-randomization exclusions.
  • Whoever generates the sequence should not be enrolling patients.
ExemplarLarge orthopaedic trials such as HEALTH (total hip arthroplasty vs hemiarthroplasty for displaced femoral neck fracture) published their protocols and analysis plans before the results. Read one before writing yours: it shows what a complete SPIRIT protocol looks like in practice.

7. Worked example: from idea to protocol

A fictional resident project, used for teaching. The protocol builder below starts with the same example.

Fictional project
An R3 wonders whether patients with surgically fixed unstable ankle fractures could bear weight as tolerated at 2 weeks rather than staying non-weight-bearing for 6 weeks.
  1. Question (F1): In adults with an unstable ankle fracture treated with ORIF (P), does weight-bearing as tolerated at 2 weeks (I), compared with protected weight-bearing to 6 weeks (C), improve ankle function at 12 months (O)?
  2. Is a full trial feasible? The mentor isn't sure how many eligible patients present each year or how many would accept randomization. Decision: a pilot RCT with feasibility outcomes (recruitment rate, retention, adherence) and prespecified progression criteria, not hypothesis testing (Eldridge 2016).
  3. Sample size: for the eventual definitive trial, a target difference based on a published MCID and an SD from prior studies (A6; the embedded calculator). The pilot's own size is justified by its feasibility objectives.
  4. Protocol and SAP: SPIRIT items, analysis populations, missing-data plan (R3) and harms (wound complications, loss of reduction).
  5. Approvals and registration: REB (F5) and ClinicalTrials.gov registration before the first patient is enrolled.
  6. Budget: research coordinator time, REDCap build, PROM licences and statistician time; apply for a departmental seed grant. [COMMITTEE: typical seed-grant size and deadline]
