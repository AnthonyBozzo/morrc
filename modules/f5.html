Why this matters to youAn REB delay is the most common reason resident projects miss Research Day. Knowing what the REB will ask lets you design a project it can approve quickly, and protects your patients and you.

1. TCPS 2 and its three core principles

Canadian institutions that receive federal research funding (CIHR, NSERC, SSHRC) must follow the Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS 2, 2022). It rests on three core principles:

PrincipleWhat it requiresOrthopaedic example
Respect for PersonsAutonomy and free, informed, ongoing consent; protection of those with diminished autonomyA surgeon-independent person approaches patients; capacity is assessed in delirious hip-fracture patients
Concern for WelfareMinimize risk, protect privacy, favourable balance of harms and benefitsDe-identified radiographs; a DSMB for a trial of a new implant
JusticeFair distribution of burdens and benefits; no unjustified exclusionNot excluding older adults or non-English speakers without a scientific reason; using interpreters and translated forms

The policy is applied proportionately: the more risk, the more scrutiny.

2. Does my project need REB review?

TCPS 2 requires REB review of research (a systematic investigation to establish facts, principles or generalizable knowledge) involving living human participants, or their identifiable information or biological materials. Exemptions are narrow (check the current TCPS 2 text):

  • Publicly available information with no reasonable expectation of privacy (Article 2.2), e.g., published registry annual reports.
  • Secondary use of anonymous information that cannot identify anyone, provided linkage or publication won't make it identifiable (Article 2.4).
  • Quality assurance and improvement, program evaluation and audits done solely for internal assessment (Article 2.5). If you plan to publish or present, ask the REB for a determination, because many will want to review it.
Rule of thumbIf you plan to present it at Research Day or publish it, and it uses patient information, assume REB review is needed and ask early. Only the REB, not the investigator, can decide that a project is exempt.

Levels of review

  • Minimal risk: the probability and magnitude of possible harms are no greater than those encountered in the aspects of everyday life that relate to the research. Most chart reviews and questionnaire studies qualify, usually for delegated review (one or a few REB members).
  • Above minimal risk: randomized surgical trials, new devices, extra invasive procedures. These go to full board review and need ongoing (continuing) review.

3. Consent: required, waived or deferred

Consent must be free (no undue influence or coercion), informed (purpose, procedures, foreseeable risks and benefits, alternatives, privacy, right to withdraw, contacts) and ongoing (re-consent when new information arises, such as a newly identified risk).

  • Undue influence is a real risk when surgeons recruit their own patients. Have a research coordinator or another team member approach patients, and make clear that care won't change if they decline.
  • Alteration or waiver of consent (Article 3.7A) may be approved when, among other conditions, the research is minimal risk, the waiver is unlikely to adversely affect participants' welfare, and the research is impracticable without it.
  • Secondary use of identifiable information without consent (Article 5.5A), as in many retrospective chart reviews, may be approved when the identifiable information is essential, obtaining consent is impracticable, privacy safeguards are appropriate, and there is no known objection from the individuals.
  • Research in individual medical emergencies (Article 3.8), such as unconscious trauma patients, may begin without prior consent under strict conditions. Consent is then sought from the participant or an authorized third party as soon as possible.

4. Privacy for chart reviews and images

  • Direct identifiers: name, medical record number, RAMQ number, address, phone, email, full-face photographs.
  • Indirect identifiers: exact dates (birth, surgery, admission), postal code, rare diagnoses or occupations, tattoos, and small cells in tables (“the one 18-year-old with a pelvic chondrosarcoma”).
  • Coded (pseudonymized) data, where a key links study IDs to patients, is still identifiable information. Store the key separately and securely, and limit who can access it.
  • Images: remove burned-in labels and DICOM header metadata before export. Clinical photos need explicit consent for publication.
  • Data minimization: collect only what the protocol needs. Age in years usually beats date of birth.
  • Quebec context: Law 25 modernized Quebec's private- and public-sector privacy laws, and Quebec has a specific act on health and social services information. Institutional rules on where data may be stored (no personal cloud drives or personal laptops) apply. [COMMITTEE: link to McGill/MUHC/CIUSSS data-storage rules]
