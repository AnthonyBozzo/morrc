1. Identifying a design: three questions

Most abstracts can be classified in under 30 seconds by asking, in order:

  1. Did the investigators assign the exposure? Yes → experimental. If allocation was random → RCT. If not (e.g., alternating days, chart number) → non-randomized controlled trial.
  2. If not, is there a comparison group? No → descriptive (case report or case series). Yes → analytic observational; go to question 3.
  3. How were participants selected, and when?
    • By exposure, then followed to outcome → cohort (prospective if enrolled before outcomes occur; retrospective if data already exist).
    • By outcome, then looked back at exposure → case-control.
    • Exposure and outcome measured at one time point → cross-sectional.

Studies that systematically search for, appraise and (optionally) pool other studies are systematic reviews and meta-analyses. They are only as good as the studies they include.

Don't trust the labelAuthors often mislabel designs: a “prospective study” that analyzes a database collected for other purposes, or a “case-control study” that is really a cohort comparing two treatments. Classify from what was actually done.

2. The designs, with orthopaedic examples

Case reports and case series

Describe outcomes in one or more treated patients without a comparison group. They are essential for new techniques, rare tumours and unexpected complications (many implant problems were first noticed this way) but cannot show that a treatment works better than an alternative.

Cross-sectional studies

Measure exposure and outcome at the same time, giving prevalence: for example, the prevalence of rotator cuff tears on imaging in people without symptoms, or a survey of how surgeons manage femoral neck fractures. They can't establish which came first. Most diagnostic accuracy studies are cross-sectional in structure.

Case-control studies

Start with people who have the outcome (cases) and people who don't (controls), and compare past exposures. They are efficient for rare outcomes such as atypical femoral fractures or periprosthetic infection. Because investigators choose the case-to-control ratio, incidence can't be computed, so the effect measure is the odds ratio. They are vulnerable to selection of controls and to recall bias.

Cohort studies

Start with an exposure (smokers vs non-smokers, cemented vs uncemented stems) and follow forward to outcomes, giving incidence and relative risk. Prospective cohorts enrol before outcomes occur and allow standardized measurement. Retrospective cohorts (chart reviews, registries, administrative data) are fast and cheap but rely on data collected for other purposes. Both are vulnerable to confounding: exposed and unexposed patients often differ in ways that also affect outcomes (module F3).

Randomized controlled trials

Allocation by chance balances known and unknown prognostic factors on average, which is the unique strength of randomization. Good trials also conceal allocation, blind whoever can be blinded (at least outcome assessors), and follow nearly everyone. Canadian surgeons have led many landmark orthopaedic trials, for example the COTS clavicle trial (JBJS Am 2007), FAITH (Lancet 2017) and HEALTH (NEJM 2019).

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

A protocol-driven, reproducible search, selection, appraisal and synthesis of all relevant studies. Meta-analysis is the statistical pooling step. A meta-analysis of biased studies gives a precise, biased answer (module A4).

4. JBJS levels of evidence

In 2003, JBJS Am began requiring a level of evidence for every clinical article (Wright, Swiontkowski and Heckman). Separate tables exist for therapeutic, prognostic, diagnostic and economic studies, and the journal has updated them since; always check the current JBJS table. A simplified version of the therapeutic column:

LevelTherapeutic studies (simplified)
IHigh-quality randomized controlled trial; systematic review of Level I RCTs with consistent results
IILesser-quality RCT (e.g., <80% follow-up, no blinding, improper randomization); prospective comparative study; systematic review of Level II studies
IIICase-control study; retrospective comparative study; systematic review of Level III studies
IVCase series
VExpert opinion
Limits of levelsA level describes design, not execution or relevance. It is assigned per study rather than per outcome, it ignores precision and consistency, and it says nothing about whether the result applies to your patient. Use levels as a quick sort, never as a verdict.

5. Hierarchy is not certainty

The GRADE approach (Guyatt et al., BMJ 2008) rates the certainty of a body of evidence for each outcome. Randomized evidence starts at high and observational evidence at low, but then:

  • Rate down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.
  • Rate up observational evidence for a large effect, a dose–response gradient, or when plausible confounding would have reduced the observed effect.

So three small, unblinded, underpowered RCTs with a subjective outcome may give low certainty, while very large, consistent observational effects can reach moderate certainty. Module A4 teaches GRADE in full.

Discussion piece: parachutes

Smith and Pell's satirical review (BMJ 2003) found no randomized trials of parachutes and concluded that their effectiveness rests on observational data alone. The point is that dramatic effects don't need trials. Fifteen years later, a real parachute RCT (Yeh et al., BMJ 2018) found no difference in death or injury, because participants jumped from aircraft parked on the ground. The lesson is that a randomized result applies only to the population studied (here, people at no risk), and applicability is a separate judgment.

Most orthopaedic interventions have modest effects, which is exactly where confounding, placebo effects and natural history can masquerade as treatment benefit.

6. When is an RCT needed, and when is it not feasible?

Trials have overturned confident beliefs in orthopaedics. Arthroscopic debridement for knee osteoarthritis was no better than sham surgery (Moseley et al., NEJM 2002) or than physiotherapy and medical care alone (Kirkley et al., NEJM 2008). Arthroscopic partial meniscectomy for degenerative tears was no better than sham surgery (FIDELITY, NEJM 2013). Subacromial decompression gave no clinically important benefit over diagnostic arthroscopy alone (CSAW, Lancet 2018). Large uncontrolled series had suggested otherwise.

RCTs may be unnecessary or infeasible when effects are dramatic (parachutes, reduction of a dislocated hip), when outcomes are rare or delayed (implant failures years later, best studied in registries), when there is no equipoise, or when the condition is very rare (some sarcomas), where prospective registries and multicentre cohorts are the practical alternative. Glasziou and colleagues (BMJ 2007) discuss when observational evidence is enough.

Making surgical trials feasible: multicentre networks, expertise-based designs, pragmatic trials, and blinded outcome adjudication (module A1).
