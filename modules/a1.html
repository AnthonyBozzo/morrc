A1. Randomized trials in surgery

Surgical trials are harder than drug trials: surgeons can't be blinded, skill varies, and learning curves bend the results. Learn to appraise them, and to design them well.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Appraise a surgical RCT for validity, results and applicability.
  • Explain options for blinding, including sham surgery and blinded assessors.
  • Describe expertise-based trials and when to use them.
  • Explain surgical learning curves and their effect on trial results.

Prerequisites: F2, F3 and F4. Not locked: you can start here.

Why this matters to youMost practice-changing evidence in orthopaedics comes from surgical RCTs, many of them led by Canadian surgeons. Appraising them well is the core skill of Tier 2 and of the journal club you will lead.

1. What makes surgical trials different

  • Complex interventions: the “treatment” includes the approach, the implant, surgeon skill, anaesthesia and rehabilitation. Standardize what matters and record the rest.
  • Equipoise must exist in the surgical community and in individual surgeons. Surgeons often have strong preferences, which hampers recruitment.
  • Blinding of surgeons is impossible, and of patients often difficult.
  • Learning curves and expertise differ between procedures and between surgeons.
  • Crossovers (e.g., non-operative to operative) are common and complicate analysis.

The Users' Guide to the orthopaedic literature (Bhandari, Guyatt and Swiontkowski, JBJS Am 2001) frames appraisal as three questions: Are the results valid? What are the results? Will they help me care for my patients?

2. Randomization and allocation concealment

Randomization makes groups comparable only if no one can predict or subvert the next allocation. Good practice in multicentre surgical trials:

  • A computer-generated sequence, often in blocks of varying size (fixed small blocks can be guessed), stratified by centre and important prognostic factors (e.g., open vs closed fracture).
  • Central web-based allocation after eligibility and consent are confirmed. Sealed envelopes are a weaker fallback, vulnerable to tampering.
  • Randomize as close as possible to the intervention (sometimes intraoperatively, once eligibility is confirmed) to reduce post-randomization exclusions.

3. Blinding, sham surgery and expertise-based trials

PatientsSometimesIdentical incisions and dressings; sham procedures
SurgeonsAlmost neverStandardize co-interventions instead
Outcome assessorsAlmost alwaysIndependent assessors; covering scars; self-completed PROMs collected by blinded staff
AdjudicatorsYesCentral committee reviewing reoperation and union events without allocation
Data analystsYesGroups coded A/B until analysis is complete

Sham-controlled trials such as FIDELITY (partial meniscectomy, NEJM 2013) and CSAW (subacromial decompression, Lancet 2018; it compared decompression, investigational arthroscopy only, and no treatment) have shown that placebo effects in surgery can be substantial. Sham surgery is ethically defensible only with genuine uncertainty, minimal sham risk and full consent.

Expertise-based RCTs (Devereaux et al., BMJ 2005) randomize patients to surgeons who perform only the procedure they are expert in and believe in. This reduces differential-expertise bias, procedural crossovers and surgeon reluctance. The trade-off is that surgeon and procedure effects are confounded, and the design needs enough surgeons in each arm.

4. Learning curves, surgeon volume and the IDEAL framework

If surgeons are early on the learning curve for the new procedure but experienced in the old one, a trial underestimates the new procedure's mature effect, and vice versa. Remedies: require a minimum case volume before enrolment, record surgeon experience, and analyze outcomes over time or by surgeon.

The IDEAL framework (McCulloch et al., Lancet 2009) describes the stages of surgical innovation: Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment (the RCT), and Long-term study (registries and surveillance). It warns against both premature RCTs and the uncontrolled spread of untested innovations.

5. Pragmatic vs explanatory, and intention-to-treat

Explanatory trials ask whether an intervention works under ideal conditions (selected patients and surgeons, strict protocol). Pragmatic trials ask whether it works in usual practice (broad eligibility, many centres, patient-important outcomes). The PRECIS-2 tool (Loudon et al., BMJ 2015) rates nine domains on this spectrum, from eligibility and recruitment to the primary analysis.

  • Intention-to-treat (ITT): analyze patients in the groups to which they were randomized. This preserves the balance created by randomization and is the standard primary analysis for superiority trials.
  • Per-protocol / as-treated analyses break randomization: patients who cross over differ systematically. Use them as sensitivity analyses (and, with care, as part of non-inferiority analyses; A6).
Orthopaedic exemplars

Large multicentre trials led from Canada show these principles in practice. SPRINT (reamed vs unreamed tibial nailing, JBJS Am 2008) used central randomization, blinded adjudication of reoperations and standardized perioperative care, and limited early reoperation for delayed union to reduce variation in surgeons' reoperation thresholds. FAITH (Lancet 2017) and HEALTH (NEJM 2019) used international networks to reach sample sizes no single centre could.

6. Worked appraisal: lessons from SPRINT

The SPRINT trial (JBJS Am 2008) compared reamed with unreamed intramedullary nailing of tibial shaft fractures in more than 1,300 patients at centres in Canada, the United States and the Netherlands. Its design choices are a model for surgical trials:

Problem in surgical trialsHow SPRINT addressed it
Reoperation is a surgeon's decision, so it is open to bias when surgeons know the allocationReoperations for nonunion were not permitted before 6 months, and an adjudication committee blinded to allocation reviewed outcome events
Small single-centre trials are underpowered for event outcomesA large multicentre sample powered for a clinically important composite outcome
Prognosis differs by fracture typeRandomization stratified by centre and by open vs closed fracture, with the subgroup analysis prespecified
Allocation can be subvertedCentral, concealed randomization

The trial found a possible benefit of reamed nailing in closed fractures and no difference in open fractures. Note how the authors' cautious wording (“possible benefit”) matches a subgroup finding, even a prespecified one (A4, subgroup credibility).

Multicentre networks

Canadian surgeons have led collaborative trial groups that make large surgical RCTs possible. For example, the Canadian Orthopaedic Trauma Society trial of plate fixation vs nonoperative treatment for displaced midshaft clavicle fractures (JBJS Am 2007) changed practice. Networks share protocols, central randomization, data management and adjudication, and give residents a route into high-quality research.

Applying the results to your patient

  • Would your patient have met the eligibility criteria? How many screened patients were excluded, and why?
  • Were the surgeons' expertise and the implants similar to yours?
  • Are the outcomes ones your patient cares about, measured at a meaningful time point?
  • Do the absolute benefits outweigh the harms and costs (F4: NNT and NNH)?
