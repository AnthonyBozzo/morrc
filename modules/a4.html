Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Appraise a systematic review and meta-analysis.
  • Interpret forest plots, heterogeneity (I²) and funnel plots.
  • Rate certainty of evidence using GRADE.
  • Recognize redundant and low-quality meta-analyses.

Prerequisites: F3, F4 and A1. Not locked: you can start here.

Why this matters to youGuidelines and many journal-club papers rest on meta-analyses, and orthopaedics produces many small, overlapping ones. GRADE gives you a disciplined bottom line for the journal club you will lead (Tier 2 capstone).

1. What makes a review systematic

  1. A focused question (PICO) and a protocol registered in advance (PROSPERO), reported with PRISMA-P.
  2. A comprehensive, reproducible search of several databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CENTRAL) plus registries and grey literature, ideally designed with a librarian.
  3. Duplicate, independent screening and data extraction.
  4. Risk-of-bias assessment of each study (RoB 2, ROBINS-I, QUADAS-2).
  5. Appropriate synthesis (meta-analysis only when studies are similar enough) and certainty rating (GRADE).
  6. Reporting with PRISMA 2020; appraise reviews with AMSTAR 2.
Redundant meta-analysesIoannidis (Milbank Q 2016) documented a flood of redundant, overlapping and often conflicted meta-analyses. Before reading, or writing, one, check how many already exist on the same question.

2. Reading a forest plot

  • Each square is one study's estimate; its size reflects its weight, and the horizontal line is its 95% CI.
  • The vertical line at RR = 1 (or a mean difference of 0) is no effect. Check which side favours the intervention.
  • The diamond is the pooled estimate; its width is the pooled CI.
  • Ratio measures are plotted on a log scale, so RR 0.5 and RR 2 are equidistant from 1.

Heterogeneity

  • Eyeball first: do the CIs overlap? Do the point estimates sit on both sides of 1?
  • Cochran's Q tests whether differences exceed chance; it has low power with few studies.
  • I² (Higgins et al., BMJ 2003) is the percentage of variability due to heterogeneity rather than chance. Rough guide: around 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% high. Its uncertainty is large with few studies.
  • τ² estimates the between-study variance used by random-effects models.

Fixed vs random effects

A fixed-effect model assumes one true effect and weights studies by precision. A random-effects model assumes the true effects vary, gives relatively more weight to small studies and produces wider CIs when heterogeneity is present. When the two disagree, investigate why the studies differ; don't just pick the model you like.

3. Subgroups and publication bias

Subgroup credibility (Sun et al., BMJ 2010): believe a subgroup effect more if it was prespecified with a hypothesized direction, it is one of few tested, it is supported by a significant test of interaction, it is consistent across studies, and it is biologically plausible.

Publication and small-study bias: small negative studies are less likely to be published. A funnel plot (effect against precision) that is asymmetric, with small studies showing larger benefits, raises concern. With fewer than about 10 studies, funnel-plot tests are unreliable.

4. GRADE: certainty of evidence

GRADE (Guyatt et al., BMJ 2008) rates certainty per outcome as high, moderate, low or very low. RCT evidence starts high; observational evidence starts low.

Risk of biasDo most of the data come from studies at high risk?Large effect (e.g., RR > 2 or < 0.5)
InconsistencyUnexplained heterogeneity in direction or size?Dose–response gradient

ImprecisionDoes the CI include both important benefit and no effect (or harm)? Too few events?

Each “serious” concern usually rates down one level and “very serious” two. A summary-of-findings table shows, for each outcome, the relative effect, absolute effects per 1,000 patients, the number of participants and studies, and the certainty rating.
