A3. Diagnostic accuracy studies

Sensitivity, specificity and, most usefully, likelihood ratios: how a test result should change what you believe, and how to tell whether a diagnostic study can be trusted.

Why this matters to youEvery scaphoid MRI, PJI work-up and physical-exam manoeuvre is a diagnostic decision. Likelihood ratios let you move from a study's numbers to your patient's probability in seconds.

1. The 2×2 table

Target condition present (reference standard +)Absent (reference standard −)
Index test +a = true positives (TP)b = false positives (FP)
Index test −c = false negatives (FN)d = true negatives (TN)
  • Sensitivity = a/(a+c): proportion of people with the condition who test positive. A highly sensitive test, when negative, rules the condition out (“SnNout”).
  • Specificity = d/(b+d): proportion without the condition who test negative. A highly specific test, when positive, rules it in (“SpPin”).
  • PPV = a/(a+b) and NPV = d/(c+d) depend on prevalence. The same MRI has a lower PPV in a low-prevalence clinic than in a referral centre.

2. Likelihood ratios and post-test probability

LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity); LR− = (1 − sensitivity) / specificity. LRs don't depend on prevalence, and they convert your pre-test probability into a post-test probability:

Bayes in three stepspre-test odds = p / (1 − p) → post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR → post-test probability = odds / (1 + odds). The Fagan nomogram (NEJM 1975) does this graphically.
LR+LR−Change in probability (Users' Guides rule of thumb)
> 10< 0.1Large, often conclusive
5–100.1–0.2Moderate

Worked example (illustrative numbers)

A test has sensitivity 90% and specificity 85%, so LR+ = 0.90/0.15 = 6 and LR− = 0.10/0.85 = 0.12. With a pre-test probability of 20%: pre-test odds = 0.25; after a positive test, odds = 0.25 × 6 = 1.5, so the post-test probability is 1.5/2.5 = 60%. After a negative test, odds = 0.25 × 0.12 = 0.03, so the probability is about 3%.

3. ROC curves, AUC and thresholds

For continuous tests (CRP, synovial white cell count), each threshold gives a different sensitivity/specificity pair. The ROC curve plots sensitivity against 1 − specificity across thresholds; the area under the curve (AUC) summarizes discrimination (0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect). The best threshold depends on the consequences: in a PJI work-up, missing an infection (FN) may be worse than an unnecessary aspiration (FP). Thresholds chosen to optimize results in the same dataset overestimate performance and need external validation.

4. Biases in diagnostic studies, and QUADAS-2

BiasWhat happensEffect
Spectrum bias (two-gate or case-control sampling)Obvious cases are compared with healthy controls instead of consecutive patients with suspected diseaseInflates sensitivity and specificity
Partial verification (work-up) biasOnly test-positives get the reference standardInflates sensitivity, deflates specificity
Differential verificationPositives get MRI and negatives get clinical follow-upUnpredictable distortion
Incorporation biasThe index test is part of the reference standard (e.g., a marker included in the PJI definition)Inflates accuracy
Review biasIndex test and reference standard interpreted with knowledge of each otherInflates agreement

QUADAS-2 (Whiting et al., Ann Intern Med 2011) assesses four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each gets a risk-of-bias judgment, and the first three also get an applicability judgment. Report diagnostic studies with STARD 2015.

5. Orthopaedic applications

  • Suspected scaphoid fracture with normal radiographs: most such patients don't have a fracture, so the decision hinges on whether early MRI or CT changes management compared with immobilization and repeat imaging. Reference standards vary between studies (MRI, CT, follow-up imaging), which affects accuracy estimates.
  • Periprosthetic joint infection: no single test is perfect. Consensus definitions (e.g., the MSIS/ICM criteria) combine serology, synovial markers, cultures and histology, and serve as reference standards. Beware incorporation bias when a test is evaluated against a definition that includes it.
  • Physical examination: tests such as Lachman or McMurray have accuracies that vary widely across studies, partly from spectrum differences (acute vs chronic, primary care vs sports clinic) and unblinded examiners.

6. Worked example: serial tests and decision thresholds

Diagnostic tests are only useful if they move the probability of disease across a threshold where management changes. Pauker and Kassirer (NEJM 1980) described two thresholds:

  • Below the testing threshold, the disease is unlikely enough that you neither test nor treat.
  • Above the treatment threshold, it is likely enough that you treat without further testing.
  • In between, test, and choose a test whose LRs can push the probability past one of the thresholds.

Illustrative numbers chosen for teaching; they are not published accuracy estimates.

Painful total knee arthroplasty, two years after surgery
The surgeon judges the pre-test probability of periprosthetic joint infection to be 20%.
  1. Pre-test odds = 0.20 / 0.80 = 0.25.
  2. A serum marker is positive (LR+ 5): post-test odds = 0.25 × 5 = 1.25; probability = 1.25 / 2.25 = 56%.
  3. An aspirate synovial test is then positive (LR+ 4): odds = 1.25 × 4 = 5; probability = 5 / 6 = 83%.
  4. Had the aspirate been negative (LR− 0.1): odds = 1.25 × 0.1 = 0.125; probability = 0.125 / 1.125 = 11%.
Caution: chaining LRsMultiplying LRs one after another assumes the tests are conditionally independent (given infection status, one result tells you nothing about the other). Two inflammatory markers often rise together, so chaining their LRs overstates certainty. Prefer LRs from studies that evaluated the test combination, or a validated scoring system.

Reading a diagnostic accuracy paper

  1. Find the STARD flow diagram: how many eligible patients received the index test and the reference standard, and how many results were indeterminate?
  2. Check that patients were consecutive or random, with the clinical uncertainty you face (QUADAS-2, patient selection).
  3. Were index-test readers blinded to the reference standard, and was the threshold prespecified? Thresholds chosen after looking at the data overestimate accuracy.
  4. Convert the reported sensitivity and specificity into LRs, and apply them to your patient's pre-test probability.
