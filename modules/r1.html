Why this matters to youTools such as SORG and PATHFx already inform decisions about skeletal metastases, and many resident projects now involve machine learning. Knowing what discrimination, calibration and external validation mean protects patients, and your publications.

1. What prediction models do

  • Diagnostic models estimate the probability that a condition is present now (e.g., an AI algorithm flagging a scaphoid fracture).
  • Prognostic models estimate the probability of a future outcome (e.g., survival at 3 and 12 months with skeletal metastases, used to choose between more and less durable fixation; 90-day complications after arthroplasty).
  • A model's life cycle is development → internal validation (bootstrapping or cross-validation to estimate optimism) → external validation in new patients, places and time periods → impact assessment (does using it improve decisions and outcomes?) → monitoring and updating.
Development is not validationPerformance measured in the data used to build a model is almost always optimistic. Ask where, when and in whom the model was externally validated, and whether those patients resemble yours.

2. Discrimination, calibration and clinical utility

PropertyQuestionMeasures
DiscriminationDoes the model give higher risks to patients who have the outcome than to those who don't?C-statistic / AUC (0.5 = chance)
CalibrationDo predicted risks match observed risks? (Of patients predicted 30%, do about 30% have the outcome?)Calibration-in-the-large (observed/expected), calibration slope, calibration plot
Clinical utilityDoes using the model lead to better decisions than treating all or none?Decision curve analysis (net benefit)

Van Calster et al. called calibration the “Achilles heel” of predictive analytics (BMC Med 2019). A model can rank patients perfectly (high AUC) yet systematically over- or under-predict risk, which misleads every individual decision based on its numbers. The interactive demo below shows that AUC doesn't change when calibration breaks.

Decision curve analysis (Vickers and Elkin, Med Decis Making 2006) plots net benefit across threshold probabilities, weighing true positives against false positives at the threshold where a clinician would act.

3. Overfitting, sample size and common pitfalls

  • Overfitting: too many candidate predictors for too few events. The old “10 events per variable” rule has been replaced by formal minimum sample-size calculations (Riley et al., BMJ 2020).
  • Dichotomizing continuous predictors (age > 65) throws away information.
  • Data-driven selection (stepwise by p-value) produces unstable models. Penalized regression (LASSO, ridge) and prespecified predictors are better.
  • Missing data: complete-case analysis discards patients and can bias the model. Multiple imputation is usually preferred (R3).
  • Predictors not available at the moment of prediction (e.g., using a postoperative variable in a preoperative model).

Machine-learning specific

  • Data leakage: information from the test set leaking into training, e.g., images of the same patient in both, or normalization or imputation on the full dataset before splitting. Leakage inflates performance dramatically.
  • Accuracy with imbalanced outcomes: if 5% have the outcome, a model predicting “no” for everyone is 95% accurate. Report AUC, calibration, sensitivity and specificity at relevant thresholds.
  • Reference standard for imaging AI: who labelled the images, how, and were they blinded? (A3, QUADAS-2.)
  • Shortcut learning: models can learn site-specific artefacts (scanner type, burned-in labels) instead of pathology, so they fail at new sites.

Liu et al.'s Users' Guide (JAMA 2019) gives a structured approach to reading machine-learning papers. Report models with TRIPOD+AI and assess risk of bias with PROBAST (participants, predictors, outcome, analysis).

4. Worked context: survival models in skeletal metastases

For a patient with an impending pathological femoral fracture, expected survival influences whether a surgeon chooses intramedullary nailing or a more durable endoprosthetic reconstruction. Tools such as PATHFx and the SORG machine-learning algorithms estimate survival from clinical and laboratory variables. Ask of any such tool:

  1. Was it externally validated in a population like ours (tumour mix, era of systemic therapy, country)?
  2. Is calibration reported (not only AUC), and how does it hold at the decision thresholds?
  3. How are missing laboratory values handled when the tool is used?
  4. Has anyone shown that using it improves decisions?

Look up the published development and external-validation studies of these tools and compare their reported calibration. That makes a good journal-club exercise.

5. Worked example: does the model help decisions?

Discrimination and calibration don't tell you whether using a model leads to better decisions. Net benefit, the quantity plotted in decision curve analysis, does. At a threshold probability pt (the risk at which you would act), it counts true positives and penalizes false positives by the odds of the threshold:

Net benefit = TP/n − (FP/n) × pt/(1 − pt)

Illustrative numbers for teaching.

Predicting a complication after surgery
1,000 patients; 200 (20%) develop the complication. A clinician would start a preventive intervention at a predicted risk of 10% or more, so pt = 0.10 and the penalty weight is 0.10/0.90 = 0.111.
  • Model (flags 580 patients above 10%: 180 true positives, 400 false positives): 180/1000 − (400/1000) × 0.111 = 0.180 − 0.044 = 0.136.
  • Treat everyone: 200/1000 − (800/1000) × 0.111 = 0.200 − 0.089 = 0.111.
  • Treat no one: 0.
At this threshold the model beats both default strategies: the difference of about 0.024 is equivalent to about 24 extra true positives per 1,000 patients with no increase in false positives. A decision curve repeats this across a range of reasonable thresholds.

6. Imaging deep learning, fairness and dataset shift

  • Deep learning on radiographs can match or assist clinicians in tasks such as fracture detection (e.g., Lindsey et al., PNAS 2018). Ask whether the study tested the model the way it will be used (as an aid to clinicians, on consecutive patients) or only on curated images.
  • Generalization: Zech and colleagues (PLoS Med 2018) showed that a chest-radiograph model performed worse at hospitals it hadn't been trained on, partly because it could recognize which hospital an image came from. External validation at new sites is essential.
  • Dataset shift: performance drifts when the population, scanners, implants, coding practices or treatments change after development. Deployed models need monitoring and periodic recalibration.
  • Fairness: models can encode existing inequities. Obermeyer and colleagues (Science 2019) showed that an algorithm using health-care costs as a proxy for health need systematically underestimated the needs of Black patients. Check performance and calibration within subgroups (sex, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status) and whether the outcome label is a fair proxy for what matters.
Appraisal shortcutFor any AI paper, ask: What exactly is predicted, for whom, and at what moment? Was it externally validated? Is calibration shown? Is there evidence that it changes decisions or outcomes? Then work through TRIPOD+AI and the JAMA Users' Guide (Liu et al. 2019).
