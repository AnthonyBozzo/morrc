Why this matters to youMost resident projects are observational. The difference between a publishable cohort study and a misleading one is usually in how time zero, confounders and outcomes were defined, and that is decided before you open the database.

1. When observational evidence is the right tool

Observational designs are essential when trials are infeasible or unethical: rare or delayed harms, long-term implant survival, exposures that can't be randomized (smoking, obesity, diabetes), and real-world effectiveness after a trial. Their central weakness is confounding (F3). Their strengths are size, generalizability and long follow-up.

Appraising a cohort or case-control study

  • Were exposed and unexposed (or cases and controls) drawn from the same source population?
  • Were exposure and outcome measured the same way in both groups, ideally blind to the other?
  • Was follow-up long enough and complete?
  • Were important confounders identified in advance (ideally with a DAG), measured well and adjusted for?
  • Is the effect large, consistent and dose-responsive enough to survive plausible residual confounding?

2. Methods to address confounding

MethodHow it worksWatch for
RestrictionLimit the cohort (e.g., only primary THA for osteoarthritis)Reduced generalizability
Multivariable regressionEstimates the exposure effect holding included covariates constantToo few events per variable; adjusting for mediators or colliders

Instrumental variables and other quasi-experimentsExploit variation unrelated to prognosis (e.g., surgeon or hospital preference)Strong, often untestable assumptions

Austin (Multivariate Behav Res 2011) gives an accessible introduction to PS methods. None of them can fix confounders you didn't measure, such as frailty, surgeon judgment or bone quality.

3. Getting time right: immortal time bias

Immortal time bias (Suissa, Am J Epidemiol 2008) arises when exposure is defined by something that happens after cohort entry. Exposed patients must survive event-free long enough to become exposed, so that “immortal” time is credited to the exposed group.

Example

A study defines “treated” hip-fracture patients as those who filled an osteoporosis prescription within 6 months of discharge, and reports far lower mortality in that group. Patients who died in the first weeks never had the chance to fill a prescription and were counted as untreated. Fix: start follow-up for everyone at the same time zero and treat exposure as time-varying, or use a landmark analysis.

Related traps: prevalent-user bias (including long-term users who have “survived” early harms) and misaligned time zero between groups.

4. Arthroplasty registries and administrative data

National registries, such as the Canadian Joint Replacement Registry (CJRR, run by CIHI), the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry (AOANJRR) and the UK National Joint Registry, have identified poorly performing implants that trials were too small to detect.

Pitfalls

  • Revision is the usual outcome, but it captures only failures that are revised. A painful implant in a frail patient may never be revised, and revision thresholds vary by surgeon.
  • Competing risk of death: in older cohorts, 1 − Kaplan–Meier overestimates cumulative revision because it treats patients who died as if they could still be revised. Competing-risk methods (cumulative incidence functions) are preferred.
  • Confounding by indication: implants are chosen for particular patients, and by surgeons and hospitals with different volumes.
  • Completeness and validation: check the registry's capture rate and whether outcomes are validated.

Administrative (health-services) data

  • Data are collected for billing, not research. Diagnosis and procedure codes can be wrong, non-specific or missing (laterality, fracture classification, PROMs).
  • Use validated coding algorithms (with reported sensitivity and PPV) and report them as RECORD requires.
  • Coding practices change over time (e.g., ICD versions), creating spurious trends.

5. Target trial emulation

Hernán and Robins (Am J Epidemiol 2016) propose designing an observational analysis by first writing the protocol of the randomized trial you would ideally run, the “target trial”, and then emulating each component with observational data:

  1. Eligibility criteria (applied at time zero, using only information available then)
  2. Treatment strategies being compared
  3. Assignment procedure (emulated by adjusting for baseline confounders)
  4. Time zero: when eligibility is met, a strategy is assigned and follow-up starts, aligned for every group
  5. Outcome and follow-up period
  6. Causal contrast (intention-to-treat or per-protocol analogue) and the analysis plan

Aligning these three things at time zero prevents immortal time and prevalent-user bias by design. Emulation doesn't solve unmeasured confounding.

6. Worked example: reading a registry revision study

When registries get it right

Registries detect poorly performing implants that trials are too small or too short to reveal. In the late 2000s, the Australian (AOANJRR) and England and Wales (NJR) registries identified unexpectedly high revision rates for some large-head metal-on-metal hip designs, contributing to a worldwide recall of one device in 2010. This is the classic strength of registries: large numbers, real-world surgeons and long follow-up.

Questions to ask of any registry comparison

  1. Is revision the right outcome? Revision captures only failures that led to reoperation. Patients with a poor result who are too frail or unwilling to have surgery are counted as successes.
  2. Competing risk of death. In older patients, many die before they could ever be revised. Kaplan–Meier estimates that treat death as censoring overestimate the cumulative risk of revision (Lacny et al., Clin Orthop Relat Res 2015); cumulative incidence functions are preferred.
  3. Confounding by indication. Surgeons choose implants for younger, more active or more complex patients. Was there adjustment for age, sex, diagnosis, ASA class and surgeon or hospital volume?
  4. Completeness and linkage. What proportion of procedures does the registry capture, and are revisions done at other hospitals or provinces linked back to the primary procedure?
  5. Multiple comparisons. Registries compare many implants; some “outliers” will arise by chance.

How strong would unmeasured confounding have to be? The E-value

The E-value (VanderWeele and Ding, Ann Intern Med 2017) is the minimum strength of association, on the risk-ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need with both the exposure and the outcome to fully explain away an observed association. For an observed RR above 1:

E-value = RR + √(RR × (RR − 1))

Hand-worked example (illustrative)
A registry analysis reports an adjusted RR of revision of 1.8 for implant X. E-value = 1.8 + √(1.8 × 0.8) = 1.8 + √1.44 = 1.8 + 1.2 = 3.0. An unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both implant choice and revision by a risk ratio of at least 3.0 each to explain away the result. For a protective RR below 1, take its reciprocal first. Compute the E-value for the confidence limit closest to 1 as well.
