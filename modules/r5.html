Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Write a competitive conference abstract.
  • Design an effective poster and a 5–7 minute podium talk.
  • Handle questions from the floor.
  • Plan submissions to COA, OTA, AAOS and subspecialty meetings.

Prerequisites: R4. Not locked: you can start here.

Why this matters to youYour R5 capstone is a Research Day presentation, and every national meeting is a chance to be noticed by fellowship directors. Communication skills turn good research into impact.

1. Writing a competitive conference abstract

Abstract reviewers typically score: importance of the question, originality, appropriateness of methods, results with real data, conclusions supported by the data, and clarity. Practical rules:

  • Follow the meeting's format and word or character limit exactly. [COMMITTEE: link to current COA Annual Meeting abstract guidelines and deadline]
  • Never write “results will be presented”. Submit when you have results.
  • Quantify everything: numbers, effect sizes with 95% CIs, and follow-up completeness.
  • One clear conclusion that matches the primary result (no spin, A6).
  • Check each meeting's rules on prior presentation or publication, and on registration for trials.

2. Slide design

  • One message per slide. Use the headline as a full-sentence assertion (“Early mobilization reduced stiffness by 8 percentage points”), with the evidence (a figure) below it.
  • Minimal text, large fonts and high contrast. If you read your slides aloud, the audience stops listening.
  • Show data clearly: simple bar or dot plots with CIs, labelled directly, no 3D effects, chartjunk or decorative pie charts (Tufte's “data–ink” principle).
  • Tables: show only the numbers you will talk about and highlight the key one.
  • Use a consistent template and slide numbers, with a disclosure slide where the meeting requires one.
  • Images: fully de-identified radiographs and photographs (F5).

3. The 5 to 7 minute podium talk

PartTime (6-minute talk)Slides
Title and disclosures15 s1–2
Background and question1 min1–2
Methods1.5 min1–2
Results (primary first)2.5 min2–3
Limitations and conclusion / take-home message45 s1

Roughly one slide per minute. Rehearse aloud with a timer at least three times, once in front of your mentor. Moderators cut people off: finishing on time is a mark of respect.

Posters

A title readable from 2 metres, a short question, methods as a flow diagram, results as 2 or 3 large figures, and 3 bullet conclusions. Add a QR code linking to the full paper or protocol, and prepare a 60-second verbal pitch.

4. Handling questions from the floor

  1. Listen to the whole question, then briefly repeat or rephrase it (it helps the audience and buys you time).
  2. Answer directly and briefly, then stop.
  3. Acknowledge limitations honestly: “That's an important limitation. We couldn't adjust for bone density because it wasn't recorded.”
  4. If you don't know: “I don't know, but we can look at that in our data,” or defer to your mentor if they are present.
  5. With hostile or long comments, stay calm, thank them, address the core point, and offer to continue afterwards.

Prepare by anticipating the five most likely questions, usually about bias, follow-up, generalizability, the MCID and what comes next.

6. Showing your results: choosing the right figure

MessageBest figureAvoid
Who was included and lostFlow diagram (CONSORT or STROBE style)A paragraph of numbers
Difference between groups in a continuous outcomeDot plot of individual values with mean and 95% CI, or a dot-and-whisker plot“Dynamite plunger” bar charts with standard-error whiskers
Several effect estimates (e.g., adjusted ORs)Forest-style plot with a reference line, on a log scale for ratiosA dense table read aloud
Implant survival or time to reoperationKaplan–Meier curve with CIs and the number at risk below the x-axisCurves that run on after only a few patients remain
PROM change over timeLine plot of means with CIs at each time point, with the MCID markedSeparate bar charts per time point
  • Label directly on the plot instead of using a legend, and state units and time points.
  • Colour: red–green colour-vision deficiency is common, especially in men. Use colour-blind-safe palettes, and don't rely on colour alone to separate groups.
  • Axes: start bar charts at zero; avoid dual y-axes and 3D effects. Keep the same scale when comparing panels.
  • Show uncertainty: a point estimate without a CI invites over-interpretation (F4).

7. Worked example: from Research Day to publication

A fictional resident's pathway, used for teaching.

Fictional pathway
An R4 completes a STROBE-aligned cohort study on early weight-bearing after ankle fixation.
  1. Months 0–1: final analysis; mentor and statistician sign off the primary result. The 250-word abstract is drafted from the STROBE checklist, with the primary outcome, effect size and 95% CI.
  2. Month 2: submitted to Research Day and, after checking each meeting's prior-presentation rules, to the COA Annual Meeting.
  3. Month 5: Research Day podium talk; questions from judges reveal that loss to follow-up needs a sensitivity analysis, which is added.
  4. Month 7: accepted as a podium presentation at the national meeting; manuscript submitted in parallel (R4), disclosing the prior conference presentation in the cover letter.
  5. Month 12: paper revised after peer review and accepted.

Two lessons: write the manuscript while the talk is fresh (many conference abstracts are never published as full papers), and treat every audience question as free peer review.
